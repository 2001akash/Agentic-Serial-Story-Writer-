# Episode 1: Dead Drop

Alex tightened the strap on his battered messenger bag as the rain began to drum against the city’s cracked sidewalks. The courier app blinked: 17 addresses, all in the same block, all on the same street—Avenue B. He’d been riding these routes for months, but today something felt off. He pulled a stack of envelopes from the bag, each stamped with a different name and a building number that matched the same old brick façade of the 1920s‑era complex that had stood since the city’s early boom. He’d seen the building before; its windows were all dark, its doors locked, but the sign still read “Building 17.”

His first drop was a small, unmarked parcel for a woman named Evelyn Lark, an art curator who lived on the fourth floor. The keyhole was a rusty old thing; he turned the key he’d been given by the dispatch office and slipped the parcel into the slot. A faint click echoed, and a cold draft rushed out. Alex felt a chill that had nothing to do with the weather.

When he walked back, the next address—Maya Patel, a local journalist—was already boarded up. He tried the door; it wouldn’t budge. He pressed his ear to the wood and heard a faint, almost inaudible sigh. He ran the rest of the route, dropping packages at empty doorways and boarded windows. Every address on the list was either locked or abandoned. He checked the delivery app: the names matched the building’s resident list, but the dates of death on the city records were all in 2023.

He stopped at the front door of the building, a heavy oak door with a brass plaque that read “Building 17.” He pressed his palm against it, and the plaque seemed to pulse faintly. He pulled his phone out and scrolled through the city’s public records. The list of residents matched the names on his packages, but each had a death certificate dated the same day—September 12, 2023. He stared at the screen, heart pounding. The app’s notification pinged: “Delivery completed.” He didn’t have time to read the message. He was already turning to leave.

As he rode away, the rain softened into mist. A sudden thought struck him: the delivery route, the names, the dates—all a pattern. He could not shake the feeling that the city’s bureaucracy had somehow been orchestrated to line up these deaths. He imagined the building’s empty rooms, the ghostly presence of those who had once lived there. He wondered if the route itself was a list of ghosts.

The last drop, a small box addressed to a man named Samuel Ortiz, was left at the back door of a unit that had been vacant for years. He pulled the box out of the slot, and the latch squealed. Inside, a folded newspaper clipping read: *“Building 17: A Legacy of Tragedy.”* He flipped it over, his breath catching on a headline: *“Unsolved Fire Claims 120 Lives.”* The photo under the headline showed the same building, but the basement was a dark void.

Alex’s phone buzzed. A message from Maya: *“I know the building. It’s not just a rumor. We need to talk.”* He stared at the screen, the image of the building reflected in the rain‑slick pavement. He felt the weight of the unanswered questions settle over him like a shroud. He pushed his bike into the rain, the city lights flickering behind him.

Is the delivery route a list of ghosts? The question hung in the air, as tangible as the mist that clung to the street.
