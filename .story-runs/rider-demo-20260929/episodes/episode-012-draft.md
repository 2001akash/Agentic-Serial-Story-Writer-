# Episode 12: Silent Witness

Maya chose the café because its tables sat beside a broad front window. She wanted daylight, witnesses, and a quick way out. Between them lay photocopies: the old cold-case reports, the 1927 maintenance note, and Reyes’s image of the pressure marks on his logged copy.

“I’ll investigate what these documents can prove,” she said. “I’m not agreeing that a ghost wrote them.”

Alex nodded. “I didn’t ask you to.”

She tapped the 1927 note. “The archive stamp checks out, but the copy could have been altered later. Reyes taking it home doesn’t prove who wrote the message.”

“He thought the same thing.”

“Good. Then we agree on something.” Maya moved to the cold-case reports. Three deaths, filed years after the fire, all on September 12. Each report had been reclassified as an accident. “This is a pattern worth checking. It isn’t proof of a Keeper society.”

“Hargrove says old staff told stories about a group called the Keepers.”

“Stories,” she repeated. “Did he ever see a meeting?”

“No.”

“Then that goes in the rumor column.” She wrote it down anyway.

Alex showed her the returned funeral program from the archive. Maya studied the date—September 12, 1923—and the printed line stating the service had taken place in a west lobby. Her skepticism sharpened into concentration.

“The public plans don’t show a west lobby,” she said. “I can check the original permits and compare the archive copy. If the room existed, someone removed it from the official record.”

“And if it didn’t?”

“Then we find out who forged the program.” She photographed the page, careful not to take it from him. “Either way, I’m investigating.”

Outside, rain stippled the glass. In its reflection, Alex saw a line of people crossing the café behind Maya. They wore dark clothes and carried a small, closed coffin. At the end of the procession walked a boy with one soot-blackened cheek.

Alex turned around. The café door had not opened. No one stood behind them.

“What is it?” Maya asked.

He looked back to the window. The procession was gone, but the boy’s hand remained in the reflection, pressed against the glass. In his palm lay a brass number tag.

Maya followed Alex’s gaze. She saw only rain and the street beyond it.

“You saw something,” she said, not quite a question.

Alex couldn’t make himself answer.

Maya gathered the photocopies. “I’ll check the permits. You tell me exactly what you saw, even if you think I won’t believe it.”

For once, Alex had no evidence to show her—only the empty reflection and the certainty that the boy had been looking straight at him.

Can Maya believe what Alex sees?
